Load Bearing

AI data centers, the end of flat demand, and the remaking of the utility planning order

Liam Kranenburg, 26 October 2026. No. 1, decision brief, US power and utilities. Version 1.1, updated 27 October 2026.

Read the full brief Download PDF 424 KB

Key implications

US retail electricity sales reached a record 4,058 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2025, after 14 years in which they barely moved. In times past, growth of this kind would have been absorbed by a familiar machine: utilities would forecast it, regulators would approve the plants and wires to serve it, and every customer would share the cost. Yet the machine is jamming. PJM's capacity price has risen 11-fold in two auctions, Texas has 474 gigawatts (GW) of large loads waiting to connect, and on 9 October federal regulators declined to let PJM decide whose power is cut first. KPA analysis shows why. Data centers accounted for about all US demand growth between 2014 and 2023, and the plants that would serve them now take longer to arrive than the forecasts that justify them. The question is no longer only how much to build, but who bends when the system runs short.

  • The utility does not, cannot, and will not always be able to build ahead of the load. EPRI finds the largest gas turbines now take more than five years from order to delivery. One ordered today would, on average, not run until 2031—after the end of the 2025–30 window over which utilities forecast the demand it is meant to serve.
  • The balancing role is shifting, even if temporarily, to the large loads themselves. By this brief's arithmetic, if data centers gave up 0.5% of their potential annual energy use, the existing grid could, nationally and on paper, absorb all of the data center growth utilities forecast for 2025–30. It could not absorb total growth, and in Texas forecast growth is five times that headroom. Flexibility under contract today covers about 1% of it.
  • The planning order is strained, though it could yet reinvent itself. Even the data center requests judged likely to proceed are about three times forecast data center growth. The bill, meanwhile, is landing on regions rather than host states. KPA's regression across 44 states, confirming recent independent analyses, finds that being in PJM adds about 17 percentage points to household price increases, while data center share has no significant effect. The utilities that price firmness and flexibility separately will set the terms of the next decade.

—October 2026

Each one is recomputed from public data or credited to whoever published it first. Filter by theme, or draw one at random.

~100%

Data centers were roughly all net US demand growth, 2014–23.

Between 2014 and 2023, US data centers increased their electricity use by about 118 TWh. Over the same nine years, total US retail sales rose by only about 110 TWh. Everything else combined was essentially flat, within 1%. Ending the comparison in 2025, data centers account for about 60% of growth.

KPA calculation on Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and EIA data. Range across estimates: 73–108%. The general point, that data centers drive growth, was published by EIA and IEA.
