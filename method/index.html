The process

Five steps, in order

  1. Pick one event and one thesis

    Each brief starts from something that just happened and makes a single claim about what it means. The title is two words; the subtitle says it plainly.

  2. Gather the public record

    Federal data, grid operator filings, company disclosures and trade press. Every source is logged with its date so it can be found again.

  3. Recompute every number in code

    Ratios, shares and correlations are rebuilt from the raw figures in scripts, so any number in the brief can be reproduced.

  4. Stress-test the findings

    Each finding is pushed with other estimates, other start and end years, and other definitions. The wording changes until it survives, which is why some findings say “on paper” or give a range.

  5. Publish the PDF

    The finished brief is about ten pages: key implications, three sections, a few charts and the sources. It goes up here unchanged.

Ground rules

What every brief holds to

No unsourced numbers

Every figure traces to a public source listed at the end of the brief.

Claims, then hedges

A finding states what the data shows, then says where it stops holding.

Section heads are claims

Each section heading is an argument, not a topic, so the contents page reads as a summary.

Charts label themselves

Labels sit on the data, not in a separate legend, and every chart carries its source.

Disclosed AI assistance

AI tools help find sources and draft the text, and each brief says so on the cover. Every number is still recomputed from public data, and Liam Kranenburg checks each brief and is responsible for it.

Independent

Not paid by anyone it writes about. The format follows 2014–15 energy decision briefs; the work is original.

House voice

How the briefs are written

Always

  • A number with a time window up front
  • One pivot per paragraph: yet, still, indeed, by contrast
  • Long, long, short sentence rhythm
  • Units spelled out the first time
  • End on an if-then and a statistic

Never

  • Hype adjectives
  • Rhetorical questions
  • Telling the reader what to do
  • A “Conclusion” heading
